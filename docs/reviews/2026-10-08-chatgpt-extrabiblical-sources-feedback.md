I’d integrate them as a **“Related ancient texts” evidence layer attached to canonical passages**, with a secondary library for deeper exploration. The Bible reader remains the center; surrounding works help answer questions about its composition, interpretation, and boundaries.

The key is to separate three relationships:

| Relationship | What the material helps investigate | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Literary and historical context** | What ideas, language, and traditions surrounded biblical authors? | 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Dead Sea Scrolls, Josephus |
| **Parallel or alternative traditions** | How did other communities preserve or develop similar stories and sayings? | Gospel of Thomas, Gospel of Peter, Gospel of Mary |
| **Reception and canonical boundaries** | Which writings were read, disputed, collected, or treated as authoritative? | Didache, 1 Clement, Barnabas, Shepherd of Hermas, ancient canon lists |

These should be overlapping tags, not rigid categories.

**Keep the integration passage-first**

When someone reads a canonical passage, offer a small, collapsed section:

> **Related ancient texts · 3 connections**  
> Compare parallel traditions, historical context, and early interpretation.

Each connection should state:

- The exact passage in the other work.
- **Why it is relevant here.**
- Whether the relationship is an explicit quotation, close parallel, shared motif, or disputed dependence.
- Its source and review status.

Opening a connection should bring up a side-by-side comparison while preserving the canonical passage and reading position. Avoid placing whole extrabiblical chapters inline or mixing these connections into ordinary Bible cross-references.

**Give every work a compact scholarly profile**

Before displaying its text, make the reader’s bearings clear:

| Field | What to distinguish |
|---|---|
| Date | Proposed composition date versus date of surviving copies |
| Authorship | Claimed author versus scholarly attribution |
| Survival | Complete work, fragment, translation, quotation, or reconstruction |
| Language | Language of the surviving witness versus proposed original language |
| Canonical status | Status within a named community and period |
| Relationship to biblical texts | Evidence for dependence, independence, or shared tradition |
| Edition | Precisely which text and translation Jot displays |

“Extrabiblical” should describe a work’s relationship to the **selected canon**, rather than imply one universal boundary. “Related ancient texts” is consequently a better navigation label than “Rejected books.”

**Make canon assessment its own focused workspace**

Add a **“Canon and collections”** view that answers questions such as:

- Which writings appear in this particular manuscript collection?
- Which books does this ancient author explicitly accept or dispute?
- What reasons are given for their status?
- How does that evidence compare with another community or period?

Keep *included in a manuscript*, *quoted*, *recommended for reading*, and *recognized as scripture* as separate facts. A single accepted/rejected badge would erase much of the history you want to reveal.

Also distinguish **a work’s historical value from its canonical status**. A noncanonical work can illuminate a canonical passage without being equally early, independent, or historically reliable.

**Start with curated connections, not a huge library**

For the first release, I would select around **six works and 20–30 carefully reviewed connections**. Choose each connection because it helps answer an existing Jot research question. Only add full texts when you have a suitable edition, rights, and reliable passage addressing.

The secondary library can then support browsing by question:

- Jesus traditions
- Jewish interpretation and apocalyptic literature
- Early Christian practice
- Formation of scriptural collections

That makes discovery purposeful without turning the homepage into a catalogue.

Technically, retain your canonical `verse_id` system and add stable work-and-passage identifiers for surrounding literature. Connect them through typed, cited relationships; don’t squeeze noncanonical works into unused Bible verse numbers. Reuse the reader’s presentation components, but make the work, edition, and witness unmistakable.

**My scope rule would be: every prominently surfaced external passage must explain what it helps us assess about the canonical text or its reception.** That gives you room for substantial depth while keeping Jot’s central purpose clear.
